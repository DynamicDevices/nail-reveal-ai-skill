## The full-hand request exposed another assumption

We had twenty single-nail designs,not twenty complete manicures. Trying to repeat
the best five showed that the job only permitted edits to two chipped nails.
The arrows alternated two canvases; the brief confirmed it. Five nails visible
on screen did not mean five editable canvases. Normal completion unlocked the
Cone Bit and a three-nail job. Its new preparation stage worked,but that did not
prove the tool could gently erase painted polish. We recorded this separately.

After completing that preparation job,the next-client route stopped at an ad

This is a real delivery limit,not something to hide behind a100-entry ledger.

The final audit also matters: the bow reads clearly,but the butterfly is abstract
and the koi is weakly recognisable. Successful commands and accepted incremental
changes do not prove every finished design is beautiful. The article should show
the uneven results as well as the strongest sea-glass/geometric/planet designs.

## How to describe the study without overstating it

The ledger counts visually inspected changes within twenty ideas,not a hundred
independent designs or a hundred complete repaints. Many baselines are simple
material/silhouettes and several accepted steps add subject-defining features.
Show those progressions so readers can distinguish construction from later
polishing. Do not sell the count as proof that a hundred already-finished
designs were substantially optimised. Beauty/significance are Briar's reasoned
judgements against the previous image,not objective scores or Alex's endorsement.

The generative work uses parameterised geometry,tool-footprint constraints,
alternative proposals and reject/restore decisions. It is not a demonstrated
autonomous aesthetic optimiser or an exhaustive search. Flat preflight catches
some geometric conflicts; the physical app still contradicts its colour and
layering assumptions. Every claim should stay tied to the retained images,
recipes and actual recorded attempts,including the cases that did not improve.

## D19: the cleanup technique that stopped working

We had treated background-colour overpainting as our practical eraser. A snowy
mountain exposed its limits. Cutting a broad plum triangle with pale green
made a sharp peak,but left the old rounded footprint as a darker halo. The
opaque sketch had predicted a clean field; the phone contradicted it.

We tried white underneath the green cleanup. That made things worse: pale path
outlines remained far outside the mountain. This was a recorded115.99-second
failed test,not a successful correction. We restored the actual original
baseline,checked it,and rebuilt with the palette reversed: plum sky and a
sea-green mountain. The dark cuts then produced clean edges on this candidate.
We did not prove the exact blending mechanism or a universal colour rule.

Snow,a warm moon,a blue lake and a thin glint completed the landscape. Those
were five accepted changes,not credit for the failed test or restoration.
The useful optimisation was to change the approach after inspecting a real
failure. These recordings don't measure a whole-workflow speedup; they show
why blindly repeating a superficially sensible cleanup would waste more time.

## D18: making the broad brush useful, and admitting an ineffective pass

The next idea was a gold perfume bottle on black: open oval frame,neck,ivory
stopper,ivory label and one ruby-red centre. Nested shapes gave us a finished
graphic without pretending the tool could draw fine lettering. Every new outer

We also tried to remove faint dark bands inside the bottle by repeating the

did not call it a fix,and stopped unchanged repetition. The bands are subtle;

are different events,and neither earns an extra improvement cycle.

## A speedup that had to earn its place

Repeated Android MOVE commands were an observed cost: a four-row cleanup mask
took23.53s. Android's existing swipe command interpolates motion inside one
invocation. The first1200ms version took8.58s but left an uneven edge. We rejected
that attractive timing. Recreating the same oval and using3600ms swipes took
18.12s with a comparably clean edge:23% less stage elapsed in this single test.
The sampled edge moved about2.5 physical pixels,so dense paths remain useful for
exact inherited positions. This is not a whole-study speedup estimate.

No daemon,new provider,Node process or privileged injector was needed. The
portable lesson is to use an existing device primitive when it works,measure
the actual output,and retain the known fallback when quality or compatibility
fails. Full fixtures,recordings,images,recipes and rejected1200ms result remain.

## D13: the cup made the ordering problem obvious

The coffee cup looks simple, but its layers interfere. The saucer's background
mask cuts into the cup; the cup's upper cleanup removes part of the steam.
The successful order was saucer first, then handle/body/coffee/upper cleanup,
then steam last. The white base was formed by dense paint rows rather than a
background cut, which would also have erased the nearby saucer. Replaying the
scene preserved quality, but the saucer trial took137 seconds of recorded video.
That is an optimisation opportunity, not evidence of a speedup.

There were two small but useful failures: sparse column turns left a scalloped
base, and a single horizontal cleanup band left a detached white cap. Filling
the full unwanted region, rather than assuming one stroke erased everything
above it, removed the cap. Required cleanup did not add accepted cycles.

The strong result was the steam: draw one broad white wavy stroke, then draw
the same path35px to the right in plum. The remaining thin curl was continuous
and tapered. This extends the koi eye lesson: broad tools can leave delicate
shapes by controlled overpainting on a known flat background. It is not proof
that every curve or texture can be restored. The next straight-mask speed idea
is still untested and must retain the known dense-path fallback.

## Concrete story material already supported

### A recognisable flag was not the same as understanding the controls

Early strokes produced single colour and French stripes, but a game completion
badge/score did not mean the lower rim was fully covered. Several left icons
were guessed to be applicator sizes. Controlled tests showed different tools,
including a locked Cone Bit. The rotating hint was not a reliable description
of the current stage. Screenshots and stage cards were needed together.

Evidence: repository `skills/nail-reveal/references/controls.md`, `recipes.md`,
`uk-flag.md`; public videos 01–05 and early tool captures.

### Held contact changed the way we painted

Stationary DOWN/hold/UP contacts could paint without motion. One test found no
visible blue dot with requested 0.05-second holds, soft dots at 0.25 seconds,
and denser dots at 1 second. Actual contact included command overhead; this did
not establish a universal minimum delay. Longer dwell mostly increased density,
not an ever-expanding spray radius. Contact position was roughly 120 pixels
below the rendered mark on the calibrated layout.

Evidence: `tests/contact-results.csv`, `paint-and-tools.md`, videos 07/08 and
the point-grid PNG. Only single samples, not a statistical timing study.

### The eraser assumption was wrong

The file and buffer visibly overlapped a fresh-polish test dot without changing
its measured blue area. The drill removed both the blue and white base coat,
exposing pink natural nail. Even a requested 0.25-second drill contact removed
more than the coloured dot. It was not a delicate colour-only eraser.
Repainting the known white background cut a controlled crescent into a blue
mark without exposing pink: matched-crop blue area fell 22,570 → 14,576 pixels.

That enabled two different design methods: strip deliberately for negative
space, or repaint the known background when it must be preserved. Do not claim
the file/buffer never do anything: only painting-stage behaviour was tested.
The Cone Bit is still untested/locked at Salon Level 1.

Evidence: `paint-and-tools.md`, cleanup before/after PNG, videos 07/08.

### Faster work meant fewer unnecessary round trips, not faster everything

An uninterrupted serpentine contact covered the blue base in 3.216 seconds of
command time on an already painted nail. The UK red layer, including selection
and six paths, took 7.19 seconds. Neither number is total nail/session time or
a matched speedup ratio. Short 900 ms swipes left endpoint gaps.

Alex proposed the persistent ADB shell and cropped image inspection. Both were
encoded as optional fast paths, not prerequisites for portability. Crop offsets
and preview scale had to remain explicit or the next touch could be misplaced.

When cropping appeared slow, the observed saved-image operations took 5–21 ms;
some interactive-output reads waited 5 seconds. Image-analysis time was not
measured separately. A trial existing in-memory cropper failed on a package
import restriction; no new daemon/dependency was installed to replace a working
millisecond crop. Later finite batches combined control, capture, pull and crop.
